# Scope-Creep Scripts (4 situations, read-aloud lines)

Scope creep is a scheduling problem wearing a politeness costume. The fix is
the same every time: welcome the idea, route it through the process, offer a
trade. These four scripts cover the situations that cover almost everything.

The universal shape (10 seconds, kind and firm):
> welcome → route → trade → write it down.

---

## Script 1 — "Can we also just..." (the mid-project addition)

**Situation:** "While you're in there, could the site also have an events
page?"

> "Happy to look at it — new ideas mid-project are normal. Give me an hour
> today to size it, and I'll send you a one-page change request: what it
> involves, what it costs, and what it does to the timeline. If the numbers
> look good, you approve it and it's in. Nothing gets built before you see
> the page."

Then actually send the CR the same day (change-request-form.md). Speed is
what makes this feel like service instead of stonewalling.

*Worked example:* CR-001, events page — sized in an hour, +$600 fixed, launch
date held, approved by email within 24 hours. Dana's reply: "that was easy."

## Script 2 — "Can it be done faster?" (the timeline squeeze)

**Situation:** "Any way we can see it by Friday instead of Monday?"

> "Two honest ways to go faster: I move something else out of the way — which
> pushes [other milestone] — or we add focused hours, which is +$[X] for a
> rush. What I can't do is compress it quietly and hand you something
> rushed without saying so. Which trade do you want — move, add hours, or
> keep the date we have?"

A timeline squeeze gets a menu, not a hero promise. The client picking from a
menu is the client owning the schedule.

## Script 3 — The silent client (contact went quiet)

**Situation:** Approvals or inputs are overdue; a milestone is at risk. (This
is creep by omission — drift replaces decisions.)

> "Circling to the top: [input] was due [date]. Without it by [date], M3
> moves to [new date] — happy either way, just tell me which. And if the
> full version is stuck, I can proceed on [assumption] and we correct it in
> review — reply 'go ahead' and I will."

Full ladder and timing: communication-cadence-playbook.md section C. The
principle: drift gets made visible and cheap to fix, in writing, every time.

## Script 4 — "Can't this small thing be free?" (the unpriced favor)

**Situation:** "It's tiny — can you just throw it in?"

> "It is small — which is exactly why I want it written down instead of
> remembered. Two options: swap it for something of similar size still on the
> list, or add it as a one-line change request at a small fixed cost. Which
> do you prefer?"

The word "free" does the damage quietly: one favor sets the price of the next
ten asks. The swap option is the gift in this script — the client keeps their
idea, you keep your scope, and the log shows the trade.

---

## The four lines to have ready in your notes app

1. "Happy to size it — you'll have a one-page change request today."
2. "Faster costs a trade: move something, add hours, or keep the date."
3. "Without [input] by [date], [milestone] moves to [date] — your call."
4. "Small things get written down too — swap or small CR, which do you prefer?"

## What creep looks like after this kit (worked-example totals)

Fieldnote × Copperline, six weeks: **two** requests raised, **one** approved
with money (+$600, CR-001), **one** declined with a written reason (CR-002),
launch date intact, and the client thanking the process in the offboarding
survey ("changes were handled without any weirdness").

Creep is not stopped by saying "no." It is stopped by making every yes cost
what it costs — visibly, kindly, in one page.

## What success looks like

- Requests arrive as questions ("can you change-request that?") because the
  routing is familiar.
- Your yeses and your nos both arrive inside 24 hours, in writing, with
  numbers.
- The CR log reads like a ledger, and the ledger adds up: scope in, money
  in, dates honest.
