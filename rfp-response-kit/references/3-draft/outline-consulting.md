# Response Outline — Consulting / Professional Services Bids

Use for: management consulting, advisory, research, evaluation, interim management,
training and organizational development. Consulting evaluations read like academic
reviewers with procurement rules: they score the *argument*. Method sections carry the
deal; the team section is read as "can these specific people do this specific work".

## Structure

**§1 Executive summary** (1 page)
The client's decision problem framed in one paragraph, the recommended way forward in
two, the three win themes with evidence, and the deliverable list with dates. Use
`executive-summary-skeleton.md`. In consulting bids the exec summary often circulates
to stakeholders who read nothing else — write it so it can be forwarded alone.

**§2 Understanding of the context and the problem** (1–2 pages)
Reframe the stated problem into the underlying decision the client must make (budgets,
governance, policy, timing), citing their documents. Show the boundary of the mandate:
what this engagement decides, and what it explicitly does not. Consultants who
redefine the mandate unilaterally scare procurement teams.

**§3 Proposed approach and methodology** (2–4 pages — the heart of the bid)
- The analytical framework: how you will break the question down, and why this frame
- Phases with purpose, methods per phase (interviews, analysis, workshops, modeling),
  and the output of each phase
- What you need from the client per phase (data, access, decisions) — the dependency
  list from the library
- Alternatives considered and why rejected — one paragraph here reads as rigor and
  pre-empts the evaluator's "did they just apply their standard product?"

**§4 Workplan and deliverables** (1–2 pages)
Deliverable table: name, format, date offset from kickoff, reviewer on the client side,
acceptance criteria. Interim milestones where the client can correct course —
evaluators distrust plans whose first feedback point is the final presentation.

**§5 Project team** (1–2 pages)
Named team with roles on this engagement, CV extracts formatted for relevance (two
engagements each, with outcomes), the day-rate mix, and who the client actually gets —
no "team of senior experts" whose seniors appear only at kickoff and closing.

**§6 Quality assurance and independence** (0.5–1 page)
Internal review of deliverables before the client sees them, conflict-of-interest
statement, data handling, and — where public sector — any required declarations.

**§7 Knowledge transfer and capability building** (0.5–1 page)
How the client organization is stronger at the end: working sessions instead of
closed-door analysis, documented methods, workshop materials theirs to reuse. Public
and third-sector buyers score this explicitly; private buyers read it as "no permanent
dependency".

**§8 Relevant experience** (1–2 pages)
Two or three engagements: client type, question, method in three lines, result, and —
the differentiator — what the client did with the work. References with consents noted.

**§9 Fees and terms** (0.5 page or cost volume)
Fee structure (fixed by phase, day-rate caps, or value-linked), expense policy, and
payment terms aligned to their fiscal mechanics.

## Word-budget discipline

Method (§3) deserves up to a third of the document; history and boilerplate deserve
almost nothing. If the page limit forces a choice between method detail and case
studies, keep method — cases are proof of pattern, method is proof for *this* problem.

## The three mistakes that sink consulting bids

1. **The standard product in a custom costume** — the framework that conveniently
   requires exactly the modules the firm sells; evaluators have seen it, and the
   alternatives-considered paragraph is your antidote.
2. **Team inflation** — pitch partners, delivery juniors; name the delivery team and
   their actual days.
3. **Findings without decisions** — workplans that end in "a report" instead of a
   decision made; anchor every phase to the choice it informs.
