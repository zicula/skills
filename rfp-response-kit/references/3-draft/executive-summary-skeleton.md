# The Executive Summary Skeleton (write it last, place it first)

The executive summary is the one page every scorer reads — and often the only page some
of them read. Its job is to let a tired evaluator
finish it and be able to argue for you in the scoring room. One page. This skeleton
fills in order, top to bottom; each block cites where its content comes from.

---

**Block 1 — Their situation, their words** (2–3 sentences)
Open with the buyer's problem and stakes, using the RFP's own vocabulary and the phrase
you mined in the win-themes playbook §1. Not "we are pleased to submit" — the buyer
knows that; open with them.

> Draft: "[BUYER] needs [OUTCOME] by [WHEN/CONSTRAINT], and the core challenge is
> [THE HARD PART, named from your matrix]."

**Block 2 — The recommendation** (2–3 sentences)
What you propose, in plain words: the offer, the shape, the timing. One sentence a
non-specialist stakeholder can repeat in a budget meeting.

> Draft: "We propose [SOLUTION SHAPE] over [TERM], delivered by [TEAM SHAPE], with
> [KEY COMMITMENT 1] and [KEY COMMITMENT 2]."

**Block 3 — The three win themes with their proof** (3 bullets, one per theme)
Each bullet: theme as a claim + the evidence in one clause + where in the document it
is expanded. Copy these from the theme table in `5-win-themes/win-themes-playbook.md`
§2 — if a theme cannot fit one bullet here, it is not yet a theme.

> Draft pattern: "[THEME]. Proof: [METRIC/REFERENCE — one clause] (see §[X])."

**Block 4 — What you commit to, measurably** (3–4 bullets)
The commitments an evaluator can lift into their scoring notes: the SLA number, the
delivery date, the measurable outcome, the price shape if pricing is visible here.
Commitments, not capabilities — a capability is what you can do, a commitment is what
you are signing up to be measured on.

**Block 5 — Why us, and why now** (2 sentences)
One sentence of proof-of-pattern (the closest comparable engagement, with its result),
one sentence of forward commitment (the named lead, the governance, the first-90-days
promise). Close cleanly — no "thank you for the opportunity" paragraph; the last line
should be a commitment, because that is the note it leaves.

---

## The mechanics

- **Length:** one page, ~450 words at standard formatting. Two pages means the document
  has no executive summary — it has two summaries in a trench coat.
- **Written last**, after the body exists to summarize and the themes are final.
  Then **rewritten once** — the second pass moves every sentence that starts with "we"
  into one that starts with the buyer or the commitment.
- **Zero unexplained adjectives.** Run the wallpaper check (win-themes §8) before
  red-team Round 3.
- **Forwardable test:** could a stakeholder forward this page alone, without the body,
  and still make a defensible case to their colleague? That is the standard.

## Before / after (one opening)

**Before:** "ABC Consulting is pleased to submit our response to your RFP for facility
management services. With over 20 years of experience and a commitment to excellence,
we are confident in our ability to exceed your expectations."

**After:** "Your RFP sets two priorities in its first page: energy costs down 15% by
FY28, and zero disruption to tenant operations during the retrofit. Both are schedule
problems wearing a facilities costume — our plan is built around the retrofit calendar
in your Appendix C, sequenced wing by wing with tenants in place, and it prices the
15% target from measured baselines in your 2025 energy audit, not from industry
averages."

The before-paragraph is about the vendor. The after-paragraph is about the buyer's
two named priorities — and it commits to a mechanism for each. That is the whole game
of this page.
