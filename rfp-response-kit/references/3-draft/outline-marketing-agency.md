# Response Outline — Marketing / Creative Agency Bids

Use for: marketing services, advertising, digital/creative, communications, retainer
or campaign RFPs. These evaluations reward evidence of thinking, not capability lists —
every buyer has seen a hundred "full-service, data-driven, creative-first" decks.
Scale the budgets for the page limit; mirror the RFP's section numbering where given.

## Structure

**§1 Executive summary** (1 page)
Their challenge restated sharper than they stated it, the strategic idea in two
sentences, the three win themes with proof, and the commercial shape. Use
`executive-summary-skeleton.md`. If the buyer remembers one page of your bid, it is
this one — write it last, rewrite it twice.

**§2 Understanding of the brand and market** (1–2 pages)
Demonstrate you did the homework: their current positioning (as you read it), the
market/audience reality, the competitive frame, and the gap this contract exists to
close. Quote their own materials — annual reports, campaign pages, the RFP's language.
This section is where agencies win or lose before any creative appears: it is the
proof you can think, not just execute.

**§3 Strategic approach** (2–3 pages)
The strategy that connects their objective to the work: audience insight → positioning
implication → channel/program logic → measurement philosophy. One spine, clearly
argued. Name what you would deprioritize — strategy that recommends everything
recommends nothing.

**§4 Creative approach and sample thinking** (2–3 pages)
Direction, not finished work: tone, territory, one worked example ("if brief X, one
expression could be Y, because Z"). Respect the RFP's rules — some buyers explicitly
penalize unsolicited spec creative; if unclear, show thinking in words and structure,
and say you will develop it in discovery. Reuse rights on prior work: only show what
you may show; note client permissions.

**§5 Team and way of working** (1–2 pages)
Named team with roles on *this* contract (the people who pitch are the people who
deliver — say so, and staff accordingly), the operating rhythm with the client's
stakeholders, approval loops, and the escalation route.

**§6 Scope, deliverables and workplan** (1–2 pages)
Deliverable table by phase with owners and dates offset from award; what "done" means
per deliverable; the change process for the inevitable pivot (agencies that cannot
scope-change cleanly are agencies that surprise clients on invoices).

**§7 Measurement and reporting** (1–2 pages)
The KPI framework tied to their objectives (brand and performance measures, baselines,
targets, review cadence), what you report monthly, and the honest statement of what
takes 2–3 quarters to move. Attribution humility scores better than dashboard cosplay.

**§8 Relevant experience and case studies** (1–2 pages)
Two or three case studies in the format: context → objective → what we did →
measurable result → relevance here. Include one campaign that underperformed and what
it changed in your method — credibility is a differentiator in creative evaluations.

**§9 Fees and commercial model** (0.5–1 page, or the separate cost volume)
Rate structure (retainer / project / hybrid), what is included and what bills
separately, media/production pass-through mechanics, and payment terms.

## Word-budget discipline

The strategic sections (§2–§4) earn their length; everything else earns brevity. Cut
boilerplate about your agency's history to a third of a page — the buyer read your
website already.

## The three mistakes that sink agency bids

1. **The capability brochure** — a services list every competitor shares; the buyer is
   buying judgment applied to their problem, evidenced in §2.
2. **Creative fireworks, strategic fog** — beautiful mock work with no argued strategy
   behind it; evaluators' scorecards ask "approach" first.
3. **Unmeasurable promises** — "breakthrough awareness" with no baseline or method;
   commit to metrics with sources and cadence instead.
