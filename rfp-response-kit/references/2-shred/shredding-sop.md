# Step 3 — Shredding the RFP (2–4 hours, the step that decides everything)

"Shredding" is bid-room slang for taking the RFP apart into its individual requirements
so that nothing can hide. Every requirement becomes a numbered row in
`shredding-matrix.xlsx` with an owner and a status. When the matrix is complete, you know
exactly what you must produce; when the matrix is all "Final", the response is done.
This is the same discipline enterprise platforms charge five figures a year for — here it
is a spreadsheet.

## Why this step decides the outcome

Evaluators score against a checklist derived from the same document you are holding.
Responses lose for one of two reasons: a requirement answered weakly, or a requirement
missed entirely — and missed is far more common. A completed matrix converts "did we
answer everything?" from a hope into a count.

## The SOP, step by step

1. **Number the document.** Work through the RFP front to back, section by section.
   Every sentence that uses *must*, *shall*, *will provide*, *should describe*, or that
   asks for a form/signature/attachment is a requirement.
2. **One row per requirement, verbatim first.** Copy the requirement text (or a tight
   excerpt) into the row. Paraphrase later if needed — the verbatim text is what the
   evaluator will check against, and it is what you re-read at review time.
3. **Record the reference.** Section and page (`§3.1, p.7`). When the buyer issues an
   amendment, this is how you find every affected row in seconds.
4. **Classify the row:**
   - `Mandatory` — pass/fail; missing one can void the whole response
   - `Desirable` — scored; this is where the win is actually decided
   - `Form` — a document to complete and sign (certifications, disclosures)
   - `Information` — context worth noting in a draft, not answerable per se
5. **Assign an owner and a target location.** Every row gets one name and the section of
   your response where the answer will live (`§4 Service model`, `Attachment C-2`).
   A requirement with no owner is a requirement on its way to being missed.
6. **Track status until "Final":** `Shredded → Assigned → Drafting → Reviewed → Final`.
   The Coverage sheet counts for you; the pre-submit review (Step 7) starts by sorting
   this column.

The workbook ships with six example rows from a fictional municipal IT-support RFP —
they show the expected level of detail; overwrite them with your own.

## The amendment loop (where small teams bleed)

Amendments arrive mid-response and quietly invalidate sections of your matrix. The rule:

1. **Log it** in the `Amendments` sheet the day it arrives — date, what changed, which
   matrix rows are affected.
2. **Update the rows** — new requirements get numbers, changed requirements get the new
   verbatim text (keep the old text in Notes), retired requirements get struck
   through, with a note.
3. **Re-read the deadline.** Amendments move dates more often than anything else; if the
   date moved, update the intake form and the calendar the same hour.

An amendment you did not log is an evaluator instruction you did not follow. Two minutes
of logging buys that safety.

## Rules that keep the matrix honest

- **Shred before you write anything.** A partially shredded RFP produces a partially
  compliant response — usually discovered at 23:40 the night before deadline.
- **Answer the question asked.** At review time, each row is checked by re-reading the
  verbatim text next to your drafted answer: same question? all parts addressed?
- **Watch for compound requirements.** "Describe your approach, staffing and reporting
  for each service area" is three requirements in a trench coat — three rows.
- **Forms are requirements too.** Signatures, certifications, portals and formatting
  rules get rows. More submissions die on logistics than on content.
- **Keep the matrix next to the draft.** The compliance reviewer (Step 7, Round 1)
  works with both windows open; that reviewer signs the matrix, which is what makes it
  a compliance record rather than a to-do list.
