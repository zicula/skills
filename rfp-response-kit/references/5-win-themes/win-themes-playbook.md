# Step 6 — The Win Themes Playbook

A win theme is a reason to award you the contract, stated in the buyer's own terms and
backed by evidence they can verify. Weak responses describe the vendor; strong responses
argue the buyer's decision. This playbook is the hour between "the matrix is shredded"
and "we start drafting" — where you decide what the response will argue.

## 1. Themes come from the document, not from your brochure

Read the RFP three times with a highlighter, hunting for three things:

- **Repeated words.** If "seamless", "at risk", "term-time", "audit" appear five times,
  they are the buyer's anxieties. A theme that speaks to a repeated word speaks to a
  fear someone in the room actually has.
- **The evaluation criteria.** Where the RFP states weights, your themes must map to
  the weighted sections — a brilliant theme pointed at an unweighted nicety scores nothing.
- **The problem behind the request.** RFPs are fossils of an internal problem. An RFP
  for "managed print services" is usually about cost surprises, print-related security
  incidents, or an expiring contract someone is anxious to renew. Write one sentence:
  *this buyer is really buying ___.*

## 2. The theme table (one row per theme, three themes is plenty)

| Theme | Buyer concern it answers (their words) | Evidence that proves it | Where it appears |
|---|---|---|---|
| [THEME 1] | [verbatim phrase from RFP] | [case study / metric / certification] | Exec summary, §3, §5 |
| [THEME 2] | [verbatim phrase] | [reference, named person] | Exec summary, §4 |
| [THEME 3] | [verbatim phrase] | [measured outcome from a like deal] | Exec summary, §6, pricing |

Rules: every theme has at least one piece of evidence that exists today; every theme
appears in at least three places, including the executive summary; no theme survives
that you cannot evidence in one sentence.

## 3. Themes are claims with proof, not adjectives

"We are a customer-centric, innovative market leader" is not a theme — it is wallpaper,
and every competitor's wallpaper says the same. A theme is a *specific* claim a specific
evaluator could repeat to their boss:

> Weak: "We have extensive experience with municipal clients."
> Theme: "We already run the same 24/7 helpdesk model you describe in §3.1, for a city
> of comparable size — 99.2% P1 response compliance over 18 months, reference available."

The second one is longer because it carries its proof. That is what makes it a theme.

## 4. Bind each theme to evidence before drafting starts

For each theme row, open the evidence file (case study, report, reference consent) and
confirm the number you intend to cite. Themes lose deals when the number in the exec
summary does not match the number in the case study annex — evaluators who catch one
mismatch discount every number you wrote.

## 5. Place themes where scoring happens

- **Executive summary:** every theme, one sentence each, first page. Evaluators form
  their score in the first ten minutes and spend the rest of the review justifying it.
- **Section openings:** the first paragraph of each major section restates the theme
  that section serves, then proves it.
- **The matrix's desirable rows:** wherever the matrix has a `Desirable` row, the answer
  opens with the theme it serves. Desirable rows are where bids are won; they are also
  the rows vendors answer with bare minimum.
- **Closing/summary section:** repeat the themes as "what you get", in the buyer's words.

## 6. Ghosting competitors — the honest version

You cannot see other bids, but you know the field's weak shapes: generalists bidding
specialist work, distant vendors, incumbents with tired service. Address the *shape*,
not a name: "transition risk is the failure mode of this contract type — here is our
staged cutover and the reference who lived through it." Do not write a sentence you would
be embarrassed to hear read aloud in a procurement office.

## 7. Before and after — a real revision pattern

**RFP requirement:** "Describe your approach to minimizing disruption during the
transition period."

**Before (correct, forgettable):**
"Our proven transition methodology minimizes disruption through careful planning and
industry best practices. Our experienced team will work closely with your staff."

**After (theme-bound, evidenced):**
"Your §2.4 names uninterrupted term-time operations as the top transition concern — we
build the plan around it. For [comparable client], we cut over 1,100 workstations in
three weekend waves with zero lost school-days; the wave plan and their reference are in
Annex B. For you, we propose the same pattern: waves timed to [your calendar], each with
a rehearsed rollback and a named engineer on site."

The after-paragraph: quotes their requirement, carries evidence, proposes the specific
mechanism. Nothing in it is true "in general" — it is all true somewhere specific.

## 8. Rewrite pass — finding your own wallpaper

After drafting, run the wallpaper check on your own text: search for *extensive*,
*proven*, *world-class*, *best-in-class*, *innovative*, *dynamic*, *premier*, *robust*.
Each hit is either deleted or replaced with the specific claim and evidence it was
pretending to be. A page with zero adjectives and five numbers beats the reverse in
every scoring room.

## 9. The one-sentence test

Before the red-team review, each theme must pass: an evaluator could read one sentence
of your exec summary and repeat it, with its proof, in their scoring notes. If the
sentence cannot survive being repeated without you in the room, sharpen it until it can.

## 10. When you have no themes

If the theme table fills with things every bidder can claim, that is data, not failure:
either the deal is a price fight (bid accordingly, or walk per the scorecard), or you do
not yet know what this buyer actually fears — which is a question for the Q&A window,
not a guess. One good buyer question ("what does continuity risk look like for you
today?") can manufacture the theme no competitor will have.
