# LOI Craft Guide — hooks, the one-ask rule, and the do/don't table

The ten letters in `loi-library.md` are skeletons. This guide is the craft that
makes one yours: how to open, what to cut, and the mistakes program officers see
weekly. Ten minutes to read; saves hours per letter.

```
  WHAT A PROGRAM OFFICER READS, IN ORDER (typical first pass, ~3 minutes)
  ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ 1. the ask amount + what it buys (the Re: line + para 6)   │
  │ 2. do we fund this org? (geography, type, size — 10 sec)   │
  │ 3. the first paragraph (the hook — does the fit read?)     │
  │ 4. one outcome number (is there evidence of delivery?)     │
  │ 5. attachments skim (is the paperwork real?)               │
  └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  Write for that reading order: ask early, fit early, evidence visible.
```

## Five opening hooks that work (with before/after)

**1. The mirror.** Reflect their priority language, then place yourself in it.
> Before: "We are writing to request support for our programs."
> After: "Your guidelines call reading 'the infrastructure of county life' — that
> sentence describes our last twelve years: 240 adults a year, learning to read
> at two library sites."

**2. The number.** Open on the local statistic that makes the problem undeniable.
> Before: "Adult illiteracy is a serious problem in our community."
> After: "One in eight Riverbend County adults reads below the level a job
> application requires (County Health Assessment, 2025). We teach those adults."

**3. The turn-away.** Open on the demand you cannot meet — the strongest small-org
evidence that exists.
> After: "Last year our intake kept a standing list of 38 adults we had no room
> for. This letter asks for the hours that shrink that list."

**4. The result.** Open on your outcome, when it is strong and evidenced.
> After: "Sixty-four percent of our goal-setting learners advanced a full reading
> level last year — measured, not estimated. The evening program behind that
> number has room for twelve more learners per cycle, if we can staff one more
> section."

**5. The relationship.** Open on the genuine history with the funder — but only
when it is genuine.
> After: "You funded our tutoring program in 2024 and met our learners at the
> spring showcase; this letter is the expansion you asked about that day."

Pick per funder: mirror for their-language-led funders, number for stat-led
panels, result for competitive general funds. Do not stack all five — one hook,
then move.

## The one-ask rule

One letter, one amount, one thing the money does. Common violations:

- **The two-ask letter:** "we request $25,000 for the expansion — or any portion
  thereof — or general support, whichever is convenient." Reviewers read this as
  an unmanaged need. Decide what you are asking for; put the other need in the
  pipeline for a different funder.
- **The wandering ask:** the amount changes mid-letter ($25K in paragraph 2,
  "approximately $18-30K" in paragraph 7). The budget workbook exists so the
  number is settled before the letter is written.
- **The inflated ask:** rounding up because "we might as well." Funders know
  typical award sizes; an ask 3x their band reads as unfamiliarity with their
  guidelines — check the band in your research notes before drafting.

## Length and format discipline

- **Their page limit is law.** When unspecified: one page, four paragraphs, at
  most two. A program officer quoted the standard bluntly: a second page is a
  claim that you could not edit.
- **Their format questions are answered in their vocabulary.** If guidelines ask
  for "target population," do not head the section "who we serve" — match their
  words so their checklist finds your answers.
- **11-12pt font, single spacing, real margins.** Fancy formatting signals
  overhead; plain competence signals program delivery.
- **One attachment beyond what they require, maximum** — the mission one-pager.
  Everything else waits for the full proposal.

## Do / Don't (the weekly mistakes)

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Name the amount in the first two sentences | Bury the ask in the final paragraph |
| Use their priority language verbatim, quoted | Paraphrase their mission back to them wrongly |
| Two local statistics, sourced, dated | Adjectives standing in for evidence ("dire," "critical") |
| One outcome number with its instrument | Three years of activity lists ("we held, we ran, we hosted") |
| A budget line the money lands on | "Funds will support our vital work" |
| Name who will do the work, with credentials | An anonymous "our team will…" |
| The honest gap (waitlist, waiting list, hours short) | Pretending capacity you do not have |
| Address a real person, title verified | "Dear Sir/Madam" or last year's program officer |
| Offer the visit, the call, the full proposal | "Feel free to contact us with any questions" as the close |
| Proofread the funder's name — twice | One letter, ten funder names, zero reads (they compare notes) |

## After you send

Log it (`1-pipeline/`), and set the follow-up: **no news two weeks before their
stated decision date** → one polite status email ("confirming receipt and asking
whether the committee needs anything further"). When the answer comes, either
email applies (`8-reporting/stewardship-emails.md` — thank-you or the feedback
ask), and the LOI recycles: a declined LOI with a "welcome to reapply" is a
full-proposal invitation with extra steps.

## From LOI to proposal without rewriting

Every letter above is a compression of your boilerplate and skeletons, so the
recycling runs the other way: when the full-proposal invitation comes, the LOI's
paragraphs expand section by section — hook becomes executive summary, need
paragraph becomes the needs statement, the ask paragraph becomes budget narrative.
Prompt 6 in `9-ai/ai-prompt-pack.md` runs the expansion; you verify every fact it
moved. An invited proposal should feel, to the reviewer, like the same
organization that wrote the letter — because it is.
