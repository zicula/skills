# Funder Research SOP — building a prospect list from public sources

This kit ships no funder database, on purpose: databases go stale the day they are
sold, and the skill of building your own list is the part that keeps working every
year. This SOP takes you from "who would fund us?" to 10-20 qualified prospects in
the tracker, using sources that cost nothing, in about three hours for the first pass.

```
  HOURS 1-2: LONGLIST (aim wide, 30-40 names)         HOUR 3: QUALIFY (down to 10-20)
  ─────────────────────────────────────────           ─────────────────────────────────
  1. Who funds orgs like ours nearby?                 For each name, 5 minutes:
  2. Who funds our program type anywhere?             · read their own guidelines page
  3. Who gave to our peers? (990s — Step 2)           · check geography / org-size window
  4. Who does our field's association list?           · note typical award size + cycle
  5. Government programs our work already fits        · verdict: tracker / watchlist / pass
```

## Step 1 — Write the matching sentence first (10 minutes)

Before any searching, write one sentence: *"We are a [what you are] in [where] doing
[what], with a budget of [$X], seeking [$Y] for [what it buys]."*

The Riverbend example: "We are a three-person adult-literacy nonprofit in Riverbend
County doing ESL tutoring and workforce reading, with a $310K budget, seeking
$15,000 for evening-class expansion."

Every search and every eligibility check in this SOP tests against this sentence.
Prospects that fail it are not research, however impressive their names.

## Step 2 — Mine the 990s of your peers (45 minutes, the core move)

Tax returns of private foundations are public, and they list every grant made.
Your peers' funders are your best prospects — they have already said yes to your
kind of work in your kind of place.

1. **List 5-8 peers**: nonprofits your size, your program type, your region. Use
   their own websites' annual reports and staff pages to know them.
2. **Find each foundation's 990-PF**: the IRS's Tax Exempt Organization Search
   (apps.irs.gov/app/eos) holds returns for most private foundations; your state's
   attorney-general charity registry and the big nonprofit-data aggregators' free
   tiers also publish them.
3. **Extract three things per relevant return**: grantee names and cities (do they
   fund in your area?), grant sizes (do they fund at your scale?), and the
   application process note if any (many say "does not accept unsolicited requests"
   — that is a verdict, and it goes to the watchlist).
4. **Record findings in the research log** (below) as you go — not later.

The same works in reverse: find one local grantee in your field, read *their*
annual report's acknowledgments page, and you often have five funders in one page.

## Step 3 — Read the funders' own words (45 minutes)

For every promising name, the funder's website answers the five qualifying questions:

| Question | Where it lives |
|---|---|
| Do they fund our geography and cause? | "What we fund" / focus areas |
| Do they fund orgs our size? | Eligibility, or the grantee list's own mix |
| At what size? | "Typical grants range…" or the grantee list amounts |
| When is the cycle? | Deadlines, or "letters accepted year-round" |
| How do we get in? | "How to apply" — LOI first? invite-only? portal? |

Copy their deadline, the process note, and one sentence of their language about
purpose into the research log, verbatim. Their language is raw material for your
LOI's opening (the craft guide shows how it becomes your hook).

Community foundations and United Ways: search their sites for recent grantee
announcements — the lists are the same evidence as a 990, fresher. Government
programs (state arts councils, humanities councils, workforce boards, department
of education): the same five questions, and note the registration lead time —
their portals commonly require setup measured in weeks; copy what their page says
into the budget workbook's Checklist sheet.

## Step 4 — Qualify into the tracker (30 minutes)

Run every surviving name through `2-go-no-go/go-no-go-scorecard.xlsx` at the speed
you can (guidelines open, five minutes per prospect — score only what you have
evidence for). Then:

- **TOTAL 70+ and gates pass** → tracker row, `Status = Researching`, Decision GO,
  with the deadline in Deadline.
- **50-69, or evidence gaps** → tracker row, Decision CONDITIONAL, the gap written
  in Notes ("no contact yet — board chair knows their board chair").
- **Below 50, or exclusions bite** → not a row. One line in the Log: name, date,
  why passed. The watchlist keeps your research honest.
- **"Does not accept unsolicited requests"** → tracker row anyway if the fit is
  excellent, Decision CONDITIONAL, Next action = find the warm path (board, peer
  org, program officer at a community event). Invitation-only is a research
  problem, not a wall.

## The research log

Keep one running file per research session (a page in your notes, or a copy of
this file's appendix). Format:

```
2026-09-26 · Riverbend Community Foundation · communityfoundation.example
  GOS program, awards $10-25K, LOI due Oct 9, budget-under-$1M window
  Their words: "reading is infrastructure" · two literacy orgs funded 2024-26
  Verdict: TRACKER G-001 · score 76 (gate 3 pending board)
```

Five lines per funder, dated. When the Log in your tracker says "which grant paid
for what," the research log says "why we asked them in the first place" — and
eighteen months from now, when a board member suggests a famous national funder,
the research log shows you checked, scored, and passed, and why.

## The refresh rhythm

Research is a season, not a saturation: run this SOP fully twice a year (the annual
calendar schedules October and March), and spot-check monthly only what changed —
new grantees announced, cycles reopened, guidelines revised. Funders change
priorities as often as orgs change plans; the tracker's CONDITIONAL rows are your
re-check list.
